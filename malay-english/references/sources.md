# Source ledger

Researched on **21 September 2026**. Primary sources were prioritized: DBP
language advice and spelling guidance, researchers' papers, university repositories,
conference proceedings and dataset owners' cards. Search snippets were followed
to fuller sources where accessible. Access depth is stated below; a citation is
not a claim that the source validates our authored examples.

The numbered rules combine established grammar, descriptive findings and original
project decisions. **Project decisions are not attributed to DBP or to a research
paper.** The worked examples are newly written. No full papers, dictionary
collections or benchmark audio are included in this package.

## Code-switching and language annotation

### S01

Treffers-Daller, Majid, Yap and Flynn (2022), [Explaining the Diversity in
Malay-English Code-Switching Patterns](https://www.mdpi.com/2226-471X/7/4/299),
*Languages* 7(4), 299. [Publisher PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/languages/languages-07-00299/article_deploy/languages-07-00299.pdf).
Full PDF inspected, especially §§2.1–2.3 and methods. Supports varied structural
patterns, function-word switching, variable noun phrases and verbal constructions.
The review combines several regions/registers; the new observational sample is
two Malaysian lecturers, not a population-wide frequency estimate. Absence in
one cited corpus is not a universal ban. Used for R04–R09, R11, R13, R16, R20,
R27, R32 and conversational interpretation.

### S02

Gupta et al. (2023), [Singaporean Conversational English-Malay Code-Switching
Speech: An Analysis Based on Code-switching Points and Part-of-Speech](https://www.colips.org/conferences/ialp2023/proceedings/papers/IALP2023_P063.pdf),
IALP, pp. 95–99. Full PDF inspected. Analysis of a selected NSC conversational
subset finds both monolingual and switched utterances and examines switch
locations/POS. Supports separate subset accounting and a range of phrase/word
switches. Its proportions are not generation targets or results for our Mesolitica set.

### S03

Sumartono and Tan (2018), [Juggling Two Languages: Malay-English Bilinguals'
Code-switching Behaviour in Singapore](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330837063_Juggling_Two_Languages_Malay-English_Bilinguals%27_Code-switching_Behavior_in_Singapore),
*The Linguistics Journal* 12(1), 108–138. Author-uploaded primary paper; abstract
and accessible methods/context inspected. Bibliography cross-checked against
[Tan's publication list](https://ntuyytan.owlstown.net/publications).
Supports intentional communicative functions and differences within the recorded
generational groups. Do not generalize those participants to all Singaporeans.

### S04

Tay, Chan, Yap and Wong (2016), [Discourse Particles in Malaysian English:
What Do They Mean?](https://doi.org/10.1163/22134379-17204002),
*Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde* 172, 479–509.
[Publisher PDF](https://brill.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/bki/172/4/article-p479_3.pdf).
Indexed primary abstract and publisher-hosted excerpts inspected; direct PDF
retrieval was blocked. Scope is Chinese Malaysian young adults' Facebook
communication. Supports contextual, multifunctional particle interpretation;
does not establish an inventory for every Malay speaker or acoustic intonation.

### S05

Smakman and Wagenaar (2013), [Discourse particles in Colloquial Singapore
English](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/weng.12033), *World Englishes*.
Primary abstract inspected. Examines particle use across Chinese, Indian and Malay
speakers. Used to avoid equating regional English particles with an exclusive
Malay-language identity. No detailed prosodic rule is inferred from its abstract.

### S06

Yeong and Tan (2014), [Language identification of code-switching sentences and
multilingual sentences of under-resourced languages by using multi structural
word information](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2014/yeong14_interspeech.html),
Interspeech, 3052–3055. [Paper](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2014/yeong14_interspeech.pdf).
Abstract and paper sections on the shared character set and `air` inspected.
Supports contextual word-level language identification and the limits of shared
spellings. Its model accuracy is not validation of our lexicon or proposed labels.

### S07

Mave, Maharjan and Solorio (2018), [Language Identification and Analysis of Code-Switched
Social Media Text](https://aclanthology.org/W18-3206/), CALCS.
Primary abstract and annotation table inspected. Provides precedent for keeping
names, mixed words and ambiguous items separate. The languages and domain differ
from Malay speech; our inventory is an explicit adaptation, not its gold standard.

## Formal grammar and spelling

PRPM is DBP's reference service. Its pages aggregate dictionary entries and dated
answers. We rely on the relevant answer, not every statement on a result page.
These are formal-language references; our application to mixed conversation is
identified as a project choice. Unless otherwise noted, the accessible indexed
answer or dictionary entry was inspected; some direct opens returned server errors.

### S08

DBP, [Hukum D-M](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=dm).
Head/modifier ordering and recognized exceptions. Used for R07; not a universal
word-order constraint on every bilingual noun phrase.

### S09

DBP, [Pedoman Umum Ejaan Bahasa Melayu](https://eseminar.dbp.gov.my/dokumen/ejaan.pdf).
Full 65-page PDF downloaded; relevant sections inspected. Printed pages 22–24
cover bound pronouns, prepositions and particles; printed p. 58 covers mixed
Malay/foreign elements joined with a hyphen. Also consulted consonant combinations
and reduplication spelling. Orthographic permission does not validate an invented
derivative's morphology or naturalness. PDF page index is one greater than the
printed page number in these sections.

### S10

DBP, [Kata bantu](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/cari1?d=175768&keyword=bantu).
Aspect and modal categories, with examples. Used for R16–R17 and the semantic
distinctions surrounding R19. These are not context-free translation pairs.

### S11

DBP, [Ialah](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=ialah), particularly
the 29 August 2024 answer, and
[adalah](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=adalah).
Distribution of formal copular/predicative linking expressions. Older answers on
the aggregated page are not entirely consistent; use the explicit newer analysis,
and do not silently formalize a colloquial transcript. Supports R06.

### S12

DBP, [Penjodoh bilangan](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=penjodoh).
Classifier selection and the warning that not every item has a required specific
classifier. Supports R10; does not settle every English insertion.

### S13

Kho Thong Eng / DBP (2023), [Antara Kata Ganda dengan Kata Jamak](https://dewanbahasa.jendeladbp.my/2023/02/27/7335/).
Article inspected. Distinguishes reduplication, plurality, quantity expressions
and lexicalized forms. Used for R11–R12. Formal redundancy judgments are not
instructions to delete actual conversational repetitions.

### S14

DBP, [Kami dan kita](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=kami+dan+kita).
First-person group reference and inclusion of those addressed. Supports R14;
special discourse uses require context rather than forced dictionary substitution.

### S15

DBP, [Bukan](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=bukan), particularly
the 18 September 2021 answer. Distinguishes `bukan`, `tidak` and conversational
`tak nak`, including contrastive exceptions. Supports R18 and scope-sensitive review.

### S16

DBP, [Pesan kan](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=pesan+kan),
17 July 2024 answer on locative `di` and `pada`; also S09 §III.F for preposition
spacing. Supports R25/R28. The keyword is a search entrypoint; consult the
specified answer rather than treating the keyword itself as the rule.

### S17

DBP, [Peminjam](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=peminjam),
27–28 February 2014 answers. Nasal-prefix alternation and initial consonants;
use alongside the separately recorded lexical forms. Supports R21/P06.
The brief answers are not a complete exception dictionary for loanwords.

### S18

DBP, [Imbuhan i](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=imbuhan+i),
5 January 2010 answer on active/passive affix combinations, and
[dia kan](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=dia+kan),
7 October 2019 discussion of argument/beneficiary constructions.
Supports R22; do not reduce the varied meanings of `-kan/-i` to one animacy rule.

### S19

DBP, [Terbaca](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=terbaca),
3 February 2010 answer responding to an example from Singapore's *Mekar* textbook.
Supports first-person object/passive ordering (R26); confirms that a legitimate
Malay construction is not an error merely because English orders it differently.

### S20

DBP, [Daripada](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=daripada).
Source, material and comparison senses, with contrasts involving `dari`.
Used for R28 and amount/source relation checks, not an automatic normalization
rule for every colloquial occurrence.

### S21

DBP, [Kata hubung](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=kata+hubung)
and [relatif](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=relatif).
Relative `yang` and types of clause linking, including complement distinctions.
Used for R29–R30. A word's ability to link clauses does not make it interchangeable
with every English conjunction.

### S22

DBP, [Kata tanya](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=kata+tanya),
12 March 2012 and 15 April 2025 answers on formal question forms;
[dengar](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=dengar), 4 September 2015
answer on requests and prohibition. Supports R19/R31. Formal written prescriptions
are not applied as automatic corrections to colloquial speech.

### S23

DBP, [Terbalik](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=175768&keyword=terbalik),
12 August 2021 answer correcting e pepet/e taling symbol confusion.
Used for P02. An older answer surfaced elsewhere with reversed symbols; it was
excluded from the adopted guidance. IPA /ə/ denotes schwa here.

### S24

DBP, [Komputer](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/Cari1?d=54140&keyword=komputer).
Malay dictionary entry and derivatives. Supports the established-loan example
in R35; it does not mean every technical word in Malay speech must be translated.

### S25

DBP, [Fail](https://prpm.dbp.gov.my/cari1?d=175768&keyword=fail).
Malay document/computer-file senses. Supports contextual differentiation from
English verb `fail`, and the spelling distinction `fail/file`.

## Pronunciation and variety

### S26

Mukhlis Abu Bakar (2019), [Sebutan Johor-Riau dan Sebutan Baku dalam Konteks
Identiti Masyarakat Melayu Singapura](https://publisher.unimas.my/ojs/index.php/ILS/article/view/1521),
*Issues in Language Studies* 8(2), 61–78.
[Full paper](https://publisher.unimas.my/ojs/index.php/ILS/article/download/1521/922/4744).
Abstract, historical/variety discussion and pronunciation comparison inspected.
Supports distinguishing pronunciation models and linguistic identity; historical
policy descriptions are dated to the paper, not asserted as a newly checked policy.

### S27

Mukhlis Abu Bakar and Maisarah Zulkifly (2023), [Perbandingan sebutan hibrid dalam
wacana lisan berskrip dan wacana lisan spontan di Singapura: Analisis fonetik](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/server/api/core/bitstreams/a1795fba-f4a8-4baa-955f-0f3b487a3147/content),
*Jurnal Bahasa* 23(2), 259–288. Full PDF obtained; abstract, scope and relevant
phonetic discussion inspected. Studies scripted speakers and spontaneous radio
speech; supports hybrid pronunciation and scoped variation, not a universal
phoneme-to-spelling rewrite.

### S28

Clynes and Deterding (2011), [Standard Malay (Brunei)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-international-phonetic-association/article/standard-malay-brunei/F08DBC925B1AC32E65E6291C487F845A),
*Journal of the International Phonetic Association* 41(2), 259–268.
[Author's companion resource](https://fass.ubd.edu.bn/research/malay/index.html).
Full publisher PDF inspected for consonants, glottal stop, vowels, connected speech
and prosody. It describes Standard Malay from a Brunei newsreader, not vernacular
Brunei Malay and not a Singapore pronunciation standard. The companion recordings
were located but not listened to in this work. Used for P04–P08 with those limits.

## Evidence limits and research decisions

- DBP establishes standard forms; descriptive papers establish that particular
  patterns occur in particular samples. Neither source type alone certifies all
  new bilingual utterances.
- We did not adopt blanket bans on function-word switching, English plurals in
  Malay frames, affixed English stems, or natural colloquial omissions. Such bans
  exceed the available evidence.
- We did not copy a Tamil suffix system, assume a single Malay accent, or convert
  Indonesian language material into an unnamed generic Malay profile.
- Online forum posts, generic grammar aggregators and newly surfaced papers with
  insufficiently checked provenance were not used as rule authority.
- Only short benchmark excerpts appear in a diagnostic note. Authored examples
  are marked as authored, and they are not represented as corpus observations.
