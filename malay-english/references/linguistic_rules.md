# Malay grammar and Malay–English switching

Rule IDs are stable lookup keys. **Standard** describes formal Malay conventions;
**descriptive** describes attested usage within a source's scope; **project** is
this package's generation or annotation choice. Mixed examples below are newly
authored unless explicitly identified otherwise. They need local-speaker review
before being treated as validated training targets. Source IDs link to the
[source ledger](sources.md).

## R01 — Select the task before changing language

**Project.** Rewriting permits language changes while preserving meaning;
transcription permits neither invented switches nor silent translation. Keep
the original alongside an edited rendering. A Malay-only recording remains
Malay-only even if the desired training corpus is mixed. Rejecting it from a
specific subset does not make the recording defective.

## R02 — Preserve meaning before increasing mixing

**Project.** Protect agency, possession, number, time, uncertainty, negation,
modality and conversational purpose. `Saya belum submit` means “I have not
submitted yet”; `Saya tak nak submit` means “I do not want to submit.” Both mix
languages but they are not interchangeable. A question must remain a question.

## R03 — Choose a community and register

**Descriptive → project.** Singapore and Malaysian findings inform separate
profiles; neither represents every speaker. Maintain the requested relationship
and situation. Use restrained conversational Malay by default, retain formal
phrasing in formal quotations, and avoid adding local place names or kinship
terms for atmosphere. Singapore conversational patterns vary across generations
in the recorded sample of [S03](sources.md#s03).

## R04 — A clause frame is a tool, not a word-count vote

**Descriptive → project.** Malay–English speech permits insertion, alternation
and more interwoven structures; a single frame is not always obvious
([S01](sources.md#s01)). For drafting, identify which construction licenses the
predicate and its arguments. `Saya perlu check jadual` uses a Malay construction;
`I need to check bila dia sampai` combines an English main clause with a Malay
embedded question. Do not assign a frame simply because one language has more tokens.

## R05 — Switch by construction, without a compulsory ratio

**Project informed by S01–S03.** Allow a meaningful word, phrase or clause switch;
do not replace every second word. Keep a phrasal verb such as `follow up` coherent.
Do not append unrelated Malay to an English sentence merely to pass a filter.
Corpus coverage targets, if supplied by an experiment, belong in its brief rather
than in universal grammar. One short insertion can be a legitimate switch.

## R06 — Malay predicates need not copy English “be”

**Standard.** Malay permits nominal, adjectival and prepositional predicates.
Formal `ialah` and `adalah` have different distributions and are not universal
translations of *is*. Do not insert them before every verb
([S11](sources.md#s11)). Authored conversational `Bilik ini quiet` does not need
`adalah`; in a full English clause use `This room is quiet`. Avoid `Saya adalah
check borang` when composing a neutral Malay-framed utterance.

## R07 — Keep modifier order local to the noun phrase

**Standard + descriptive.** Malay commonly puts the head before its modifier
([S08](sources.md#s08)); an English phrase can retain English internal order.
Compare `fail lama`, `fail lama itu`, and the English island `the old file`.
For generation prefer transparent constructions such as `draft baru itu` or
`the new draft`. These preferences do not license automatically reversing every
mixed noun phrase: variable orders are documented in S01.

## R08 — Demonstratives carry reference, not decoration

**Project application.** Preserve “this” versus “that.” In Malay-framed phrases,
`ini/itu` and conversational `ni/tu` often follow the noun: `meeting itu`.
In an English island, `this meeting` stays intact. Do not routinely produce
`this meeting ini`; if doubling occurs in a transcript, preserve and annotate it
instead of applying a generation preference as a deletion rule. S01 documents
demonstrative switching; S09 supplies spelling conventions.

## R09 — Preserve possession and distinguish possession from description

**Descriptive → project.** `Buku saya` and conversational `saya punya buku` can
express possession, while `punya` has other contextual uses. Mixed possessive
patterns are documented in S01. Authored `Ini charger saya` identifies the owner;
`Ini saya punya charger` is more colloquial. Do not turn `their complaint` into
`a complaint about them`. Never insert `punya` between every pair of nouns.

## R10 — Classifiers follow meaning and usage

**Standard → project.** Malay classifiers include `orang`, `ekor`, `buah`,
`helai` and others; no one classifier fits all nouns, and not every noun requires
one in every context ([S12](sources.md#s12)). `Dua orang reviewer` and
`dua helai kertas` illustrate different classes. For an uncertain inserted noun,
use a simple clear count construction rather than inventing a classifier.
Do not add a number or unit absent from the source.

## R11 — Number marking is not automatic English pluralization

**Standard + descriptive.** Malay can express plurality through quantity words
or reduplication; lexicalized reduplicated forms are not reducible to ordinary
plural rules ([S13](sources.md#s13)). `Tiga buku` does not require `buku-buku`.
English inserted nouns show variation in S01. Preserve an English island such
as `three files`; do not blindly append `-s` to Malay nouns or delete every
English plural after a Malay quantifier in recorded speech.

## R12 — Reduplication can change more than number

**Standard → project.** Separate grammatical reduplication, lexicalized words,
and a speaker's hesitation. `Kanak-kanak` is a lexical word; `jalan-jalan` is not
simply “many roads.” Do not normalize it to `jalan`. Use a hyphen for an ordinary
written reduplicated form; keep disfluent repetition in its own transcript layer.
See [S09](sources.md#s09), [S13](sources.md#s13).

## R13 — Pronouns encode social relationships

**Descriptive → project.** Select `saya`, `aku`, `awak`, `kau`, titles, or `I/you`
with the conversation's relationship in view. English pronoun switching is
documented; it is not automatically an error ([S01](sources.md#s01)). Do not
assume `aku/kau` is suitable for an unfamiliar customer. Do not enforce English
object-case repairs on a verbatim mixed transcript. Address preferences can vary
between speakers and settings; maintain the established profile.

## R14 — Keep inclusive and exclusive “we” distinct

**Standard + project.** In the ordinary contrast, `kita` includes the addressee
and `kami` excludes them ([S14](sources.md#s14)). Preserve that meaning when the
source resolves it: `Kita boleh start` versus `Kami boleh start`. English *we*
may be ambiguous; retain English *we* or ask for context when the distinction
matters. `Dia` alone does not identify gender; do not invent *he* or *she*.

## R15 — Short pronouns and -nya need contextual analysis

**Standard + project.** Written bound `ku-`, `kau-`, `-ku`, `-mu`, `-nya` follow
attachment conventions ([S09](sources.md#s09)); `buku saya` is a transparent
alternative when authoring ordinary conversation. `-nya` is not always a literal
third-person possessive. Do not split lexical words just because they end in
`nya`, or turn every spoken independent `kau` into a bound form. Preserve the
actual construction in transcription.

## R16 — Malay time and aspect are not English tense endings

**Standard → project.** `Sudah/dah`, `sedang`, `masih`, `pernah`, `akan` and
`belum` contribute different temporal/aspectual meanings ([S10](sources.md#s10)).
Do not mechanically add English `-ed` after `dah` or equate `sedang` with every
English *-ing* form. Authored `Saya dah submit semalam` preserves completion and
yesterday; `Saya sedang menulis the summary` preserves an ongoing event. Full
English clauses retain the tense/aspect appropriate to their meaning.

## R17 — Modality must survive the switch

**Standard → project.** `Boleh`, `dapat`, `mesti`, `perlu`, `patut`, `mahu/nak`
and `mungkin` are not interchangeable ([S10](sources.md#s10)). Keep permission,
ability, obligation, advice, desire and probability distinct. `You boleh submit`
does not mean `You mesti submit`. Do not turn “may not be ready” into a ban on
being ready. Resolve the source sense before translating English *can*, *may* or *must*.

## R18 — Negation needs the right target and scope

**Standard + conversational.** The usual distinction is `tidak/tak` with verbal
or adjectival predicates and `bukan` with nominal or prepositional predicates;
contrastive constructions allow exceptions ([S15](sources.md#s15)). Compare
`Saya tak setuju` and `Itu bukan keputusan saya`. Do not turn this useful default
into an absolute ban on `bukan` before a verb. In mixed speech, determine which
constituent is denied before proposing a repair.

## R19 — “Not yet,” “not anymore” and “don't” differ

**Project application of S10, S15 and S22.** `Belum siap` retains “not ready yet”;
`tak lagi` may convey “no longer”; prohibitive `jangan` tells someone not to act.
`Jangan delete fail itu` is an instruction, while `Saya tak delete fail itu` is a
statement. Do not discard `lagi`, `pernah`, `semua`, `hanya` or similar small words
when they control scope. Check “not all” against “all ... not.”

## R20 — Bare English verbs do not require an invented support verb

**Descriptive → project.** English verb insertions occur in Malay constructions
without obligatory English infinitival `to` or a Malay equivalent of Tamil's
light-verb pattern ([S01](sources.md#s01)). `Saya nak print borang` is a drafting
option. Do not produce `nak to print` as a mechanical translation. Conversely,
`I want to print borang itu` has an English frame. Do not ban all inflected
English forms in mixed speech; their grammatical role and the variety matter.

## R21 — Malay affixes carry structure and meaning

**Standard → project.** Check native derivations lexically: `baca → membaca`,
`tulis → menulis`, `sapu → menyapu`, `kira → mengira`. The nasal prefix has
conditioned forms, and loans or consonant clusters require care
([S17](sources.md#s17)). Do not implement “delete the first letter” globally.
Do not force an active prefix into a passive/object construction, or assume
conversational prefix omission means the reference is wrong.

## R22 — -kan and -i are not interchangeable decoration

**Standard → project.** Suffixes can change participant roles, causation or
direction toward an object ([S18](sources.md#s18), [S35](sources.md#s35)). Check the complete verb and
its arguments, not just its English gloss. Compare authored `Saya membeli buku`
with `Saya membelikan adik buku`: the beneficiary is explicit in the second.
For an uncertain mixed derivative, a clear phrase such as `Tolong print borang
itu untuk saya` is preferable to an invented affix combination.

## R23 — Other derivations are lexical, not a universal affix template

**Project with standard morphology references S17–S18 and [S34](sources.md#s34).** `Ber-`, `ter-`, `peN-`,
`-an`, and `ke-...-an` can change word class or meaning. `Terbuka` may describe
an open state; `ter-` does not always mean accidental action. `Tertinggi` is a
superlative construction. Use a verified whole word; do not attach an affix to
an arbitrary English string to make it look Malay. Preserve derivations present
in source speech even when generation would choose a simpler phrase.

## R24 — Mixed stems require a declared spelling policy

**Standard spelling + project.** DBP's spelling guide permits hyphens at a
Malay/foreign-element boundary and gives mixed affix examples
([S09](sources.md#s09), printed p. 58). This is not proof that every proposed
mixed derivative sounds natural. In authored examples, `di-check` can visibly
separate Malay passive `di-` from an English stem. Retain a benchmark's original
spacing; store any normalization separately and label internal morphemes when needed.

## R25 — Distinguish locative di from passive di-

**Standard.** A location such as `di rumah` uses a separate preposition; a passive
verb such as `ditulis` has an attached prefix ([S16](sources.md#s16)).
`Di office` describes a location; `di-check` under this package's mixed spelling
describes an action undergone. Whitespace alone cannot classify the role in a
noisy transcript. Do not concatenate every `di` with the following word.

## R26 — Preserve passive roles and the actor–verb sequence

**Standard → project.** Malay object/first-person patterns such as `Borang itu
saya isi` are legitimate, not reversed English ([S19](sources.md#s19)). Formal
passive construction choices differ with actor type. Authored `The form sudah
saya isi` preserves the completed action and its actor. Do not rewrite it so
that the form fills the speaker. A transcript may contain colloquial constructions
outside a formal guide; annotate rather than silently standardizing them.

## R27 — Kena can signal obligation or an affected participant

**Descriptive → project.** `Kena` has multiple constructions, including modal
and passive-like uses in mixed speech ([S01](sources.md#s01)). Authored `Saya
kena submit hari ini` concerns obligation; `Saya kena scam` concerns being
affected. Decide who acts and who undergoes the event. Do not give a universal
`kena = must` replacement rule or infer a specific tense without context.

## R28 — Preserve spatial, source and recipient relations

**Standard → project.** Formal Malay distinguishes `di/ke`, `dari/daripada`,
and `pada/kepada` according to their roles ([S16](sources.md#s16),
[S20](sources.md#s20)). `Dari pejabat` and `daripada pengurus` express different
source relations. Preserve “at,” “to,” “from,” and “for” when switching. Spoken
`kat/dekat` needs its contextual location or proximity sense; do not mechanically
expand every `kat` to the English *near*.

## R29 — Relative yang is not a universal filler

**Standard → project.** `Yang` can connect a noun with a relative clause;
`bahawa` introduces certain complements ([S21](sources.md#s21)). Authored
`Use borang yang saya hantar semalam` identifies which form. Avoid adding `yang`
where it changes attachment or leaves the clause incomplete. In source speech,
preserve colloquial complement patterns instead of imposing formal replacements
without a separate normalization task.

## R30 — Keep clause logic and reported speech

**Project application of S21.** `Kalau/jika` preserve conditions, `sebab/kerana`
reasons, `tapi/tetapi` contrast, `supaya` purpose, and `walaupun` concession.
Do not replace an uncertain “if” with certain “when.” Keep the speaker of a quote
distinct from the narrator: `Dia kata, "Don't wait," tapi saya tunggu` retains
the reported instruction. An English quotation alone should be flagged as
quotation switching when analyzing a corpus.

## R31 — Questions and requests retain their force

**Standard + project.** Malay questions need not use English auxiliary inversion;
formal `-kah` and conversational question particles belong to different styles
([S09](sources.md#s09), [S22](sources.md#s22)). `Boleh check sekarang?` asks;
`Check sekarang` directs. Distinguish `apa`, `siapa`, `mana`, `bila`, `kenapa`
and `berapa`. Do not add a confirmation-seeking particle if the source is a neutral
information question. Colloquial `ke` must not be confused with directional `ke`.

## R32 — Adjectives and degree follow the chosen construction

**Project application.** Compare `sangat mahal`, `mahal sangat`, `lebih mahal`
and an English island `much more expensive`. Preserve the source degree, not just
its positive/negative tone. Authored `Bilik ini too noisy untuk saya` keeps an
English degree phrase. Avoid a mechanical double comparative such as `lebih
more expensive` in neutral generation; retain it if faithfully transcribing.
Mixed adjective placement is descriptive evidence in S01, not a fixed template.

## R33 — Particles change stance

**Descriptive → project.** Particle functions depend on context and sometimes
intonation. Do not append `lah` everywhere or substitute `kan`, `meh`, `lor`
and `ah` indiscriminately. Malaysian English particle research is not a universal
inventory for Malay speakers ([S04](sources.md#s04), [S05](sources.md#s05)).
Use [the register guide](spoken_malay_and_register.md) to decide whether a
particle contributes an intended appeal, check, insistence or other stance.

## R34 — Rumi spelling is not a language label

**Research + project.** The same letter sequence may occur in both languages;
word-level language identification requires context ([S06](sources.md#s06)).
`Air` can be Malay “water” or English “air”; `main` can be Malay “play” or an
English adjective. Preserve ordinary orthography and annotate meaning separately.
Do not infer English from capitalization, Latin letters or an English dictionary
hit alone. A spelling that resembles Indonesian also does not settle variety.

## R35 — Conventional loans are not automatic switch evidence

**Project informed by dictionaries and S06.** `Komputer` is an established Malay
form ([S24](sources.md#s24)); English etymology does not force an `EN` label.
`Bank`, names and acronyms can remain ambiguous/shared without context. Keep
Malay `fail` distinct from English `file`, and from English verb `fail`. A
lexicon entry offers possible interpretations rather than a global replacement.
See [lexical decisions](lexical_decisions.md).

## R36 — Keep entities, numbers and domain terms intact

**Project.** Preserve names, IDs, medication names, amounts, dates and technical
terms exactly unless the task expressly authorizes normalization. `MRT`, a brand
name or `RM20` alone does not prove an English clause. Do not localize SGD to MYR,
rename a service, infer gender from a name, or translate an identifier. Separate
a spoken abbreviation from an editor-added expansion.

## R37 — Spoken reductions are not arbitrary misspellings

**Project informed by S01 and S15.** Allow declared colloquial forms such as
`tak`, `nak`, `dah`, `ni`, `tu`, `je` and `kat` when appropriate. Do not simulate
speech by removing random letters, using digit reduplication, or adding text-message
abbreviations to an ASR reference. Formalization, pronunciation notation and
verbatim transcription are different products; keep their policies separate.

## R38 — Preserve meaningful disfluency and other languages

**Project.** Keep a real restart, filled pause or self-correction under the
transcription policy. Do not treat every `uh`, `okay` or duplicated token as
substantive English evidence. Annotate genuine third-language material rather
than forcing it into `MS` or `EN`. Unexplained foreign characters in a reference
need audio review; they are not proof that another language was spoken.

## R39 — Validate sounds separately from text

**Research → project.** Pronunciation varies across formal and conversational
Malay and across speakers ([S26](sources.md#s26), [S27](sources.md#s27)).
Correct spelling does not certify TTS language choice, affix realization or
switch timing. Read [pronunciation and ASR](pronunciation_and_asr.md), retain
spelling where pronunciation alone varies, and inspect audio before claiming
that a generated clip realizes both languages.

## R40 — Evaluation labels must be traceable

**Project.** Use the explicit labels and evidence stages in
[annotation and benchmarking](annotation_and_benchmarking.md). Preserve original
references, cleaned references, spans, uncertain decisions, clip boundaries and
review status. Report counts by category and duration. “Dataset named
code-switching,” “text contains an English-looking word,” and “audio-confirmed
Malay–English switch” are separate claims.
